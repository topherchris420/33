Historical aerospace instrumentation

What can I still trust?

Cockpit engineers have spent decades on one question: how do you turn sensors, computers, and failures into information a person can rely on? These exhibits teach that discipline with original, generic examples — then hold Project 33's own ground-status model up to the same question.

About the historical source

Historical aerospace engineering

The supplied document is the OCIP supplement to the F-117A utility flight manual (TO 1F-117A-1A, 1992). Its distribution statement limits it to U.S. Government agencies, and it states that reproduction for non-military use is not permitted.

So nothing from it is reproduced here — no text, figure, symbol, display format, procedure, or value. It informed only the choice of topics: display architecture, navigation visualisation, fault presentation, and human–machine interfaces. Every exhibit below is an original, generic illustration of practice common to aviation displays, and none is an operational or training aid.

Project 33 ground status

Project 33 research

Units from protocol/ground_status_model.json. Every dependency is pinned to firmware source lines, and tests/test_ground_status_model.py fails if a pin stops matching. A pin shows a dependency is implemented — not that it was exercised on hardware.

The shared lesson. A good architecture can tell you what it no longer knows. The generic graph does it through redundancy and reversion; Project 33's read-only status board does it by mapping each silent channel back to the units it depends on — and by listing, honestly, the capabilities the system does not have at all.
